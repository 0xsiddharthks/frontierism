# Reconstructing and testing an argument

These are analytical choices, not mandatory page headings. Select the ones that illuminate this text.

## First understand its problem

Identify the decision or puzzle as the author and original audience faced it. What alternatives were actually available? What institution, social practice, distribution, or assumption was under dispute? Distinguish the author's framing from the full option set. A pamphlet arguing for ratification is an intervention in a contest, not a neutral constitutional textbook.

State the thesis as an answer to a question. Separate description (what is), causation (why it occurs), prediction (what will happen), prescription (what ought to be done), and legitimacy (who has authority). An empirical premise does not establish a moral conclusion without an additional value judgment.

## Explain from first principles

Define only the terms doing real work. Use their historical meanings: republic, democracy, faction, sovereignty, representation, property, liberty, and federalism may differ from present usage. Avoid assuming that national/state, centralized/decentralized, or democratic/undemocratic are single scales.

Reconstruct the reasoning as a small chain of actual premises and inferences. Identify actors, information, incentives, resources, decision rules, enforcement, and feedback. Where relevant, distinguish:

- Individual advantage from collective welfare; coordination from coercion; agreement from credible commitment.
- Formal authority from implementation capacity; checks on power from means of exercising it.
- Choosing representatives from controlling them; accountability from expertise; majority rule from equal rights.
- Costs of action from costs of delay; ordinary politics from crisis incentives; short-run gains from dynamic effects.

Use a concrete thought experiment when it makes the mechanism easier to understand. Mark it as your example and spell out its limits. A modern game-theoretic restatement can illuminate an old text without implying that the author used that formal model.

## Find the crux

Name the premise or causal step on which the recommendation most depends. Ask what observation, counterexample, institutional change, or different moral priority would reverse the result. The crux should distinguish this essay from adjacent essays.

Separate evidence supplied by the text from assertion, historical analogy, deductive reasoning, and rhetorical pressure. A list of historical failures does not identify a single cause without examining alternatives. Treat predictions about a proposed institution as predictions; do not mark them verified because the institution was later adopted.

Give a serious objection in its strongest form. If using an actual opponent, read and cite that opponent; otherwise label the objection your analysis. Explain the author's explicit reply where present, then identify what remains unanswered. Do not manufacture a balanced debate by giving weak objections equal space.

## Social, political, and economic stakes

Ask who decides, who pays, who gains, who can exit, who can veto, and whose interests count in the author's category of the people. Discuss exclusions, slavery, gender, class, empire, or status when the text or necessary context makes them consequential; explain their role rather than adding a detachable moral disclaimer.

For economic reasoning, identify the actual margin: tax base, compliance, incidence, bargaining leverage, credit, trade barriers, public goods, or administrative scale. Distinguish aggregate gains from distribution and a plausible mechanism from measured effects.

For political reasoning, connect values to institutional design: which mechanism is supposed to protect liberty or improve decisions, and when might that same mechanism obstruct action or entrench power? Do not infer sovereignty, custody, consensus, or effective control from one another.

## Connections and transfer

Distinguish continuation, supporting premise, alternative mechanism, and tension. A few explained connections are more useful than dozens of tags. For a corpus, connect the essay to its immediate argumentative sequence and a small number of relevant counterparts. Never claim consistency merely because the same author wrote both.

End with a few questions that require reconstruction or application. Supply concise folded answer sketches when helpful. Good questions test whether a reader can explain the inference and its failure condition, not only recall a slogan.
