# Understanding and testing a source

Apply this method to the actual material and the user's purpose. Source format governs acquisition, not analytical depth. A philosophical essay, empirical paper, interview, narrative, and technical explanation may combine several kinds of reasoning.

## Establish its question and concepts

Identify the question, decision, puzzle, or experience the source addresses. Explain the alternatives and context that make it consequential. Distinguish the author's framing from the available alternatives; advocacy is an intervention in a dispute, and an interview may contain several positions.

Define the concepts doing real work before relying on them. Explain their historical or disciplinary meanings and any ambiguity that changes the conclusion. State the thesis as an answer to the question, where a thesis exists. Separate description, causal explanation, prediction, prescription, and legitimate authority. An empirical premise needs an additional value judgment to establish a moral recommendation.

## Explain from first principles

Reconstruct the difficult step in a small chain of premises and inferences, or an explicit mechanism when that is the source's explanatory form. Identify the relevant actors or objects, relationships, information, resources, constraints, decision rules, feedback, and consequences. Show how the proposed result follows and what must be assumed along the way.

Use a concrete example or thought experiment when it makes the mechanism easier to understand. Label it as your explanatory model and explain its limits. A modern restatement can illuminate an older argument without implying that the original author used that framework. Do not replace the source's actual reasoning with a more familiar argument.

For technical exposition, trace the relevant inputs, state, transformations, and failure conditions. For narrative or history, distinguish the sequence described from the explanation of why it occurred. Do not invent a formal argument or an opposing speaker merely to fill a template.

## Match claims to evidence

For empirical research, explain the question, study design/data, population, measures, comparison, result, and scope. Preserve units, denominators, dates, uncertainty, and estimate-versus-observation status. Distinguish correlation, identified causal effects, and a proposed mechanism. Assess selection, measurement, alternative explanations, external validity, and whether wider conclusions exceed the result when those issues matter.

For interviews and debates, reconstruct each consequential speaker's position, support, qualifications, and replies. Retain attribution uncertainty and distinguish genuine disagreement from different definitions, questions, or scopes. Agreement among speakers does not independently verify their claims.

For philosophical and interpretive arguments, examine definitions, premises, logical steps, examples, counterexamples, historical context, and normative commitments. For historical narratives, identify the evidence behind consequential causal claims and the limits of analogy. A sequence of failures does not isolate a single cause without comparing alternatives.

Throughout, distinguish the source's claims, independently established context, your explanatory model/analogy, and your assessment. Locate consequential attributed claims in the actual source. Verify decisive factual hinges against primary or scholarly sources where feasible; state the scope of any independent checks. Treat predictions as predictions even if the proposed institution or intervention was later adopted.

## Find the crux and strongest challenge

Identify the premise, causal step, measurement, interpretation, or value priority on which the conclusion most depends. Explain what counterexample, observation, institutional change, or alternative priority would change the assessment. The crux should distinguish this source from its neighbors.

Present a serious objection, strongest limitation, or plausible alternative explanation as appropriate. Explain the source's reply where present and what remains unresolved. If attributing an objection to a real opponent, read and cite that opponent; otherwise label it your assessment. Avoid manufacturing a balanced debate or giving weak objections equal standing when the evidence is one-sided.

## Political, social, and economic lenses when relevant

Ask who decides, who pays, who gains, who can exit or veto, and whose interests count. Discuss exclusions, slavery, gender, class, empire, or status when the source or necessary context makes them consequential; explain their role in the argument. Do not add a fixed ideological checklist to unrelated material.

Explain historical uses of republic, democracy, faction, sovereignty, representation, property, liberty, and federalism in context. National/state, centralized/decentralized, and democratic/undemocratic are not single interchangeable scales. Keep historical legal arguments distinct from present law.

For institutional reasoning, distinguish individual advantage from collective welfare; coordination from coercion; formal authority from implementation capacity; selecting representatives from controlling them; expertise from accountability; majority rule from equal rights; and ordinary incentives from crisis incentives. Connect values to an actual institutional mechanism and identify when it could obstruct action or entrench power.

For economic reasoning, identify the actual margin: tax base, compliance, incidence, bargaining leverage, credit, trade barriers, public goods, or administrative scale. Separate aggregate gains from distribution, immediate effects from dynamic responses, and a plausible mechanism from measured effects. Compare real alternatives, including the costs of delay, rather than an idealized rival.

## Learning and connections

Explain what the reader can now understand or apply. When useful, include a few questions requiring reconstruction, transfer, or identification of a failure condition, with concise folded answer sketches. Keep direct explanation sufficient for understanding the source.

Propose only a few meaningful conceptual connections and explain whether they are continuation, support, alternative mechanism, or tension. In a collection draft, refer to manifest source keys and mark candidate connections provisional; the coordinator reviews both sides and applies native links. A shared author or repeated claim does not establish consistency or corroboration.
