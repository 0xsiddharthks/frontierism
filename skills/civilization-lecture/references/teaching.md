# Writing a useful lecture study page

The reader wants both fast recall and deeper understanding. Depth comes from a clear model, precise distinctions, and worthwhile questions—not the volume of paraphrase. Use the source's actual intellectual structure instead of a generic history template.

## Source fidelity

First formulate the lecture's central problem and answer in one sentence each. Trace the argument internally: what causes what, through which mechanism, and under what conditions? Identify the examples that carry the argument, counterexamples acknowledged by the lecturer, and assumptions that remain unstated.

Attribute contested generalizations and grand theories: “The lecture argues…” is different from “Historians have established…”. Do not silently promote a transcript assertion into settled fact. Distinguish:

- **Lecture argument:** an accurate account of the lecturer's view.
- **Historical context:** an independently checked statement with its own citation, when added.
- **Interpretation or question:** the agent's analytical connection, objection, or exercise.
- **Uncertain transcript or figure:** what could not be resolved from the available artifact.

Do not manufacture disagreement either. Explain the particular evidentiary limitation or contested inference rather than attaching a generic warning to every page. Correct a clear transcription error only when the surrounding source or another checked source supports the correction, and record any correction that affects meaning.

## Layered reading

A useful default, adapted to the lecture:

1. **Executive recap:** a short attributed account of the central thesis and main line of reasoning. Make the scope visible.
2. **Read the argument:** direct the learner to specific source sections or transcript anchors, with questions that reveal a mechanism, comparison, or tension. Stable local anchor IDs can locate text in a private source snapshot even when the public page offers no matching fragment links. Never invent public URL fragments.
3. **Understand the concepts:** explain unfamiliar ideas through separately attributed, fact-checked general context and original examples. This is a substantial teaching layer, not a list of unexplained terms. Clearly distinguish it from the lecturer's claims.
4. **Think it through:** original analysis that tests the argument rather than reproducing it. Ask what evidence would distinguish rival explanations, what boundary conditions matter, or how the model changes under a counterfactual. Clearly label newly introduced examples.
5. **Figures and retrieval practice:** link important source figures and ask the reader to interpret relationships actually visible in them. Recall prompts should require an explanation, comparison, causal sequence, or counterexample. Brief answer cues may help, but do not disguise a detailed transcript summary as an answer key.
6. **Sources and route onward:** source URL, lecture number, source snapshot date, raw-artifact location if appropriate for this private destination, related lectures actually checked, and previous/next links.

Use headings and short paragraphs. Native Notion toggles can hide answers or optional detail if the connector preserves them. Add only useful callouts; do not make every paragraph a callout. For unfamiliar names, places, periods, and institutions, supply enough context to follow the argument without requiring prior course knowledge. Total length is a teaching decision, not a padding target.

The deeper layer must teach the actual subject, not mostly generic methodological skepticism. Explain several necessary terms, institutions, or intellectual distinctions in plain language. For literature or religion, reading a relevant primary passage can ground this layer more effectively than repeating an interpreter's account; verify the translation's rights status. For political or economic history, show how the relevant institution or mechanism works. Keep evidence cautions precise and subordinate to understanding. A page that could fit another lecture by changing the names is not finished.

## Copyright scope

Check the rights and applicable source/output limits for this run. Default protected web content calls for a bounded attributed recap and links, not a substitute for the article. Count the aggregate source-derived output across the whole page, including paraphrased summaries of figures or source-specific definitions. Do not label source paraphrase “original analysis” to evade the limit.

Where the active tool supplies a word limit, use that limit; a conservative default for this workflow is 200 source-derived words from a protected webpage, with no more than 25 quoted words. A typical executive recap plus deeper source map of 160–190 words leaves little room for other source-derived prose. Link to diagrams instead of copying their text into a new prose summary. Independently reasoned questions and critiques must add a new analytical task, not encode the source's full answer. Additional context sources each have their own allowance; track them individually.

User-supplied source text, a verified open license, or a confirmed rights basis can change what is permissible. Record the actual basis and its scope; do not infer permission from free access. Even when extensive transformation is allowed, condense purposefully and avoid publishing unnecessary raw copies. If richer lecture-specific notes require a rights basis not established, continue with the permitted guide and make the limitation clear rather than blocking the entire course.

## Diagram decisions

Each extracted figure needs a disposition, including decorative images and repeats. Record whether it was inspected, unavailable, a duplicate, or not instructional; give a reason when omitted. A verified image description can be useful, but remains source-derived content when describing a protected lecture diagram. Use an embed only when permitted and technically supported. Otherwise link the original figure or article section. Preserve any necessary credit and distinguish the source image from an agent-created conceptual diagram.

Never claim to have inspected a figure based on its filename, surrounding text, or extraction metadata. A missing or unreadable image is a bounded limitation, not permission to guess.

## Human review questions

- Could a reader explain the lecture's main question and thesis after the recap?
- Does each attributed claim have a real source locator?
- Are source pointers specific enough to find the passage efficiently?
- Do explanations clarify the mechanism, or merely repeat labels?
- Is the conceptual background useful, fact-checked, and separately attributed?
- Are original prompts intellectually useful and clearly separated from the lecturer's view?
- Are claims of fact, theories, and uncertainty represented honestly?
- Does the page preserve the lecture's spirit without becoming a replacement transcript?
